Agentic AI Engineering Study Book

Project Deep Dives and Architecture Walkthroughs

Interview answer (say this first). A deep dive is not a feature tour; it is a structured argument that you can design under constraints. Give it a shape: the problem and who had it; the constraints (scale, latency, cost, team, deadline, compliance); the architecture on a whiteboard; one request walked end to end; two or three key decisions with the trade-offs you accepted; the outcome with numbers; and what you would change now. Interviewers do not want every detail. They want to see you choose, and they will probe the areas you skip: scale, failure, cost, security, observability, and “why not X?”. Prepare two or three projects deeply rather than ten shallowly, and rehearse the story out loud with a timer. The honest framing is: “here is the problem, here is what I chose, here is what it cost, and here is what I would do differently”. A candidate who names a trade-off and a limit is more convincing than one who claims everything went perfectly.

Note: Verified. The one runnable example on this page — the latency budget — was executed offline in plain Python (Python 3.14). The walkthrough templates were checked against the structure this book uses in Phase 11. Cross-references point to the phase that taught each topic in depth.

Why this exists

The project deep dive is the part of the interview closest to the actual job. Coding tests check a skill; the deep dive checks whether you can own a system and explain it. It is also where strong candidates lose points, because the failure modes are subtle:

  • A feature tour has no architecture. “Then I added a queue” is not a design. The interviewer wants boxes, arrows, and a reason.
  • No numbers means no scale. Without QPS, data size, latency, and cost, the interviewer cannot tell a toy from a production system.
  • No trade-offs means no judgement. Anyone can list components; the signal is what you gave up and why.
  • Vague credit destroys trust. “We built the platform” invites “what did you do?”, and the story collapses.
  • Surprise questions test the design. If you never mention failure, cost, or security, the interviewer will ask, and an unprepared answer looks like you never thought about production.
  • Memorised scripts break on follow-ups. The deep dive rewards understanding, not recall.

Here is what that looks like in practice:

  • A candidate described a RAG system for ten minutes and could not say how many documents or what the retrieval recall was.
  • A candidate claimed “we built our own vector database”, then could not explain why the managed option was rejected.
  • A candidate never mentioned failure until asked, then admitted there was no timeout on the model call.
  • A candidate presented a system that “handled millions of requests” but had no number for latency, cost, or error rate.
  • A candidate could not answer “what would you do differently?”, which read as no reflection and no growth.

The lesson: rehearse the deep dive as a structured argument with numbers and trade-offs, and prepare for the five questions interviewers always ask.

The one-sentence purpose. A deep dive proves you can turn a messy problem into a design with explicit trade-offs, and explain it to someone who was not there.

Two distinctions matter most:

  • What the system is vs how you decided. The first is a diagram; the second is the interview. Always attach a reason and a rejected alternative to each major choice.
  • Your work vs the team’s work. Be precise. “I designed the retrieval pipeline and the eval gate; a teammate built the ingestion workers” is honest and still strong.

The core idea

Think of a doctor presenting a case to a colleague. They do not recite every symptom; they give the problem, the constraints, the diagnosis, the treatment, the evidence, and what they would do differently next time. The interviewer is the colleague who will ask “why did you not try X?” and “what if the patient gets worse?”. The mental model is a consultation, not a sales pitch: structure, evidence, trade-offs, and an honest review.

The five questions to invite when you finish: How does it scale? How does it fail? What does it cost? How did you secure it? How did you know it worked? If you answer those before you are asked, you look senior. If you are caught by them, you look like you only built the happy path.

The mental model in one line. Present a system the way a doctor presents a case: here is the problem, here is my decision, here is the evidence, here is what I would change.

How it works

  1. State the problem and the user. One or two sentences: what was broken or missing, and who felt it. This frames every later decision.
  2. State the constraints before the design. Scale, latency target, cost ceiling, team size, deadline, and any compliance rule. Constraints are what make the design a judgement rather than a taste.
  3. Draw the architecture, then explain it. Put the main boxes on the whiteboard before talking: client, gateway, service, data stores, model, queue, external dependencies. Boxes first, narration second.
  4. Walk one request end to end. Trace a single user request through the boxes. This is the part that proves you understand your own system, because it exposes ordering, state, and latency.
  5. Name two or three key decisions and the rejected alternatives. “We chose a managed vector store over self-hosting because our index was under 10 million vectors and the team was two people.” Always pair a decision with what you did not choose.
  6. Give the outcome with numbers. Latency, throughput, error rate, cost per request, adoption, quality metric. If you do not have a number, say what improved qualitatively and why you could not measure it.
  7. Say what you would change now. One or two concrete improvements. This proves reflection and shows you still think about the system.
  8. Invite the hard questions. “Happy to go deeper on scaling, failure handling, cost, or security.” Then answer them with prepared material.
  9. Keep the first pass short. Two to three minutes for the structured intro, then let the interviewer steer. A twenty-minute monologue loses the room.
  10. Prepare two or three projects deeply. One big system, one AI/ML system, and one hard debugging or incident story. Reuse them across questions.
  11. Rehearse out loud with a timer. Reading a design is not the same as explaining it. Practice until the two-minute version is automatic.
  12. Build a decision log while you work. Note decisions and reasons as they happen, so the interview is recall, not reconstruction.

The walkthrough test: could the interviewer redraw your architecture from memory, and name one trade-off and one number? If yes, you explained it well.

The syntax you will use

These are the templates and forms that make a walkthrough clear. Adapt them to your own projects.

The project one-pager. Write this before the interview; it becomes the spine of every answer.

Problem:     Support agents spent 6 minutes per ticket searching 4 tools.
User:        ~300 internal agents, 1,200 tickets/day (~0.04 QPS average, ~0.2 QPS peak).
Constraints: P95 answer under 3s, data stays in-region, team of 2, 10 weeks.
Design:      gateway -> retrieval (hybrid) -> reranker -> LLM -> citations;
             queue for the batch re-index; Postgres + pgvector.
Decisions:   1) managed model API over self-host (spiky load, no GPU team);
             2) hybrid search over dense-only (rare product codes).
Outcome:     P95 TTFT ~0.5s streaming (full answer ~8.5s); groundedness 0.81 -> 0.94; 12% fewer escalations.
Change now:  shard the index per tenant from day one; add the eval gate earlier.

One page forces the structure and surfaces the gaps before an interviewer finds them.

Whiteboard conventions. A tiny legend keeps the drawing readable.

[ box ]        = a service or component
( cylinder )   = a data store (Postgres, vector index, cache)
{ diamond }    = a decision or gateway/breaker
-->  solid     = synchronous call on the request path
-.-> dashed    = asynchronous message or background job
==>  double    = batch / offline pipeline
Numbers on arrows = typical latency or rate, e.g. "80ms, 6 qps"

Label every arrow with a direction and a latency. An unlabelled arrow is a place the interviewer will probe.

Interviewers love this table. It shows the alternatives were considered, not missed.

A decision record. A short ADR you can quote, one per important choice.

# ADR-004: Route model calls through a gateway
Date: 2026-03-11
Status: accepted
Context: two teams called providers directly with their own keys and no quota.
Decision: all calls go through a gateway for auth, quota, cache, and tracing.
Consequences: one extra hop (+8ms); central policy; a new single point to operate.
Rejected: per-team keys (no quota), client-side cache (no tenant key).

The “Rejected” line is the one interviewers remember, because it proves a real choice.

A latency budget in Python. Put a number on each stage and find the dominant one.

stages = [("embed query", 15), ("vector search", 25), ("rerank", 60),
          ("prompt build", 5), ("model TTFT", 400),
          ("generation (200 tok x 40ms)", 8000), ("post-process", 20)]
total = sum(ms for _, ms in stages)                  # full response
budget = 3000
ttft = sum(ms for n, ms in stages
           if n not in ("generation (200 tok x 40ms)", "post-process"))
print("total (full response) ms:", total)          # 8525
print("over budget by ms:", total - budget)        # 5525
print("time to first token ms:", ttft)             # 505
print("generation share %:", round(8000 / total * 100, 1))       # 93.8 %

Generation is 93.8% of the 8,525 ms full response, so the fix is streaming (show tokens as they arrive) and a shorter answer, not a faster vector search. TTFT is 505 ms; the 8,525 ms total is the full answer, which is why the response is streamed. A latency budget tells the interviewer where the real bottleneck is.

Examples: simple to real

Example 1 — the 30-second elevator pitch. Lead with the problem and your role, not the technology.

"Support agents were losing six minutes per ticket searching four tools. I designed
and built a retrieval-augmented assistant that answers from those tools with
citations. I owned the retrieval pipeline and the evaluation gate; a teammate
built the ingestion jobs. We got P95 TTFT under 0.5s (streaming), groundedness from 0.81 to 0.94,
and 12% fewer escalations. The main trade-off was nightly indexing, so brand-new
docs wait a day."

Technology comes last, after the problem and the result. That order signals product sense.

Example 2 — the structured walkthrough. The full first pass, about two minutes.

"Problem: agents search four tools; six minutes per ticket; 1,200 tickets/day
(~0.04 QPS average, ~0.2 QPS peak).
Constraints: P95 under 3s, in-region data, two engineers, ten weeks.
Architecture: a gateway handles auth and quota; a retrieval service does hybrid
search over Postgres and pgvector; a reranker keeps the top 8 chunks; the model
answers grounded on those chunks and returns citations. A nightly job re-indexes.
One request: gateway (10ms) -> embed (15ms) -> search (25ms) -> rerank (60ms)
-> model TTFT (400ms) -> stream tokens -> citations. 
Key decisions: managed model API over self-hosting, because load was spiky and we
had no GPU on-call; hybrid search over dense-only, because product codes need
exact match. The cost we accepted: per-token price and an extra index to maintain.
Outcome: P95 TTFT ~0.5s streaming (full answer ~8.5s), groundedness 0.81 -> 0.94, 12% fewer escalations.
What I'd change: shard the index per tenant from day one and add the eval gate
two weeks earlier. Happy to go deeper on scaling, failure, cost, or security."

Every sentence carries information. No feature history.

Example 3 — whiteboarding “how does it scale to 10x?” Answer with numbers and the next bottleneck.

"Today: 1,200 tickets/day (~0.2 QPS peak), 4 TB index, 4 retrieval pods, P95 TTFT ~0.5s.
At 10x: 12,000 tickets/day (~2 QPS peak). Retrieval pods scale horizontally to ~40; the shared Postgres
becomes the bottleneck first, so I would shard by tenant and move hot tenants to
their own replica. The model side scales with a gateway and per-tenant quota; at
10x the per-token cost dominates, so I would route easy queries to a smaller model
and cache more. The first thing that breaks is the single write path for re-indexing;
I would move it to a streaming pipeline with backpressure."

Name the first bottleneck and the next one. “It scales horizontally” alone is not an answer.

Example 4 — “Why not fine-tune?” The why-not-X question rewards a real reason and a condition.

"We considered fine-tuning to cut cost and latency. We rejected it for three
reasons: the knowledge changes weekly, so we would retrain constantly; we had
only about 300 labelled examples, too few for a reliable fine-tune; and the
answers needed citations, which a base model grounded on retrieved text gives
more naturally. If the task had been a stable format with thousands of labels,
fine-tuning would have won. We kept the option open by logging prompts and
answers so we could build a training set later."

The last sentence shows you kept the option and planned for it. That is senior.

Example 5 — handling the failure, cost, security, and observability barrage. Short, prepared answers.

Failure:      "Model calls have a 3s timeout, one retry with jitter, and a circuit
              breaker. When the breaker opens we serve the retrieved passages with
              a label instead of a generated answer."
Cost:         "About $0.004 per query; 60% is generation. Caching and routing easy
              queries to a small model cut spend 35%."
Security:     "Tenant id is in every cache key and every index filter; per-tenant
              quota at the gateway; prompts and outputs are PII-scrubbed in logs."
Observability:"We trace retrieval, rerank, and generation separately; dashboards
              show P95 latency, groundedness sampled daily, and error ratio."

Four short answers, each with a number or a concrete control.

Example 6 — “What would you do differently?” The reflection answer should be specific and already acted on.

"Two things. First, I would shard the index by tenant from day one; adding it
later cost us a migration and a weekend of backfill. Second, I would build the
evaluation gate before the first feature, not after, because we shipped two
regressions that a 150-case set would have caught. I now put a draft eval set in
the first sprint of any AI project. On the model, I would still choose the managed
API at that volume; that trade-off held up."

Naming one thing you would keep is as important as naming what you would change. It shows the decision was sound, not lucky.

In production

  • Structure beats detail. Problem, constraints, design, trade-offs, outcome, reflection. Without the spine, detail is noise.
  • Numbers or it did not happen. QPS, data size, P95, error rate, cost per request, quality metric. One real number is worth ten adjectives.
  • Every major decision needs a rejected alternative. “We used X” is weak; “we chose X over Y because Z, accepting cost C” is strong.
  • Draw before you talk. Boxes and labelled arrows on the whiteboard keep you and the interviewer aligned.
  • Walk one request. It exposes assumptions and proves you know the system, not just the diagram.
  • Answer the five hard questions before you are asked. Scale, failure, cost, security, observability. Volunteering them looks senior.
  • Prepare two or three projects, not ten. Depth on a real system beats a shallow list.
  • Use “I” for your decisions and “we” for team execution. Vague credit reads as no contribution; over-claiming reads as arrogance.
  • Keep the first pass to two or three minutes. Then let the interviewer steer into the areas they care about.
  • Own the limits. Say what the system cannot do. A candidate who names a failure mode is more trusted than one who claims none exists.
  • Have a decision log. Reconstructing choices under pressure produces vague answers; a written log produces specifics.
  • Rehearse out loud, timed. The two-minute version should be automatic so you can spend your attention on the follow-ups.

The walkthrough test. After you finish, the interviewer should be able to name one decision, one trade-off, one number, and one thing you would change. If any is missing, the walkthrough is incomplete.

Interview questions

1. Walk me through a project you are proud of, end to end.

Answer. Give the structured first pass: the problem and the user, the constraints, the architecture, one request walked through it, two or three key decisions with the rejected options, the outcome with numbers, and what you would change. Keep it to two to three minutes, then invite deeper questions. Lead with the problem, not the technology.

Follow-up: “What was your specific contribution?” Be precise. Name the parts you designed or built and the parts a teammate owned. Specific ownership is stronger than a broad claim.

Trap. A feature tour in the order you built things. The interviewer wants the design and the decisions, not the project timeline.

2. Whiteboard that architecture for me.

Answer. Draw boxes first: client, gateway, service, data stores, model, queue, external dependencies. Label every arrow with direction and typical latency. Then walk one request through it. Keep the drawing readable and erase before adding, rather than crowding the board.

Follow-up: “Which box is the single point of failure?” Point to it and say the mitigation: replication, a queue to absorb the failure, a fallback, or a circuit breaker. Never claim there is no single point of failure.

Trap. Drawing twenty boxes. A clear five-box diagram explains more than a dense one, and the interviewer scores clarity.

3. How does it scale to ten times the load?

Answer. Start from today’s numbers, multiply, and find the first bottleneck. Compute the new QPS, data size, and cost, then say what breaks first: the shared database, the write path, the model quota, or the cache. Give the scaling step for each layer and name the one you would do first.

Follow-up: “What does 10x cost?” Estimate from your current cost per request and say which layer dominates. Cost scaling is part of scaling, not a separate topic.

Trap. “It scales horizontally.” That is a property, not an answer. Name the bottleneck and the next one after it.

4. What happens when a component fails?

Answer. Pick the most important dependency — usually the model provider — and describe the failure path: timeout, retry with jitter and a budget, circuit breaker, then graceful degradation such as a cached answer or the retrieved sources with a label. State the blast radius and how you contained it with bulkheads or per-tenant isolation.

Follow-up: “How did you test the failure path?” Describe fault injection in staging or a game day. An untested fallback is a hope, and saying so shows maturity.

Trap. Describing only the happy path. Interviewers ask about failure precisely to see whether you built for it.

5. How did you control cost?

Answer. Give the cost per request and the biggest contributor, then the levers you used: caching keyed by tenant and prompt, routing easy requests to a smaller model, right-sizing and quantizing for utilisation, and trimming context. Mention budgets and quotas, and the quality metric you watched to make sure the cuts were safe.

Follow-up: “Did cost cutting hurt quality?” Have one example where you measured it. If quality dipped, say how you caught it and what you reverted.

Trap. Quoting only the monthly bill. A cost answer needs the per-request number and the trade-off, or it sounds like luck.

6. How did you secure the system and isolate tenant data?

Answer. Cover the boundaries: authentication and per-tenant quota at the gateway, tenant id in every cache key and index filter, least-privilege credentials for each service, secret management, and encryption in transit and at rest. For AI specifically, mention prompt-injection defences, output validation, and PII scrubbing in logs and traces.

Follow-up: “What is the worst case if one tenant is compromised?” Name the blast radius and the control that limits it, such as per-tenant namespaces or dedicated resources for high-tier tenants.

Trap. Treating security as a later phase. Saying “we would add that later” signals you have not run a multi-tenant system.

7. How did you know it was working in production?

Answer. Name the metrics and traces: latency by stage, error ratio, retrieval recall, sampled groundedness, cost per request, and the business metric such as escalation rate. Describe the alerting, the error budget, and one regression the evaluation gate caught before release.

Follow-up: “How did you catch a quality regression?” Describe offline evals in CI plus sampled online judging, and a specific time it fired. That combination is the expected answer at senior level.

Trap. “We watched the logs.” Observability is metrics, traces, and quality signals, not tailing a log file.

8. Why did you choose X over Y, and what would you do differently now?

Answer. For the decision, give the context, the options, the reason, and the cost you accepted. For the reflection, name one or two concrete changes, ideally ones you have already applied, and one decision you would keep. Specificity and honesty matter more than a dramatic reversal.

Follow-up: “When would you choose Y instead?” State the condition that would flip the decision, such as volume, a stable format, or a stricter residency rule. That shows the choice was reasoned, not dogmatic.

Trap. “I would not change anything.” Every real system has a lesson; claiming otherwise reads as no reflection or no honesty.

Remember this

  • Structure is the answer: problem, constraints, design, request walk, decisions, numbers, what you would change.
  • Every decision needs a rejected alternative. “We chose X over Y because Z, accepting cost C” is the sentence to rehearse.
  • Numbers make it real. QPS, data size, P95, error rate, cost per request, and a quality metric.
  • Volunteer scale, failure, cost, security, and observability before you are asked; that is what senior looks like.
  • Prepare two or three projects deeply and own your part. Precision about your contribution beats a broad, vague claim.
